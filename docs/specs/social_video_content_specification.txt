SOCIAL VIDEO CONTENT SPECIFICATION

Version: 0.1
Status: Experimental / Living Standard
Primary Medium: Social-first vertical video
Purpose: Establish repeatable editorial, writing, evidence, visual, production, and provenance standards for social content.

⸻

1. PURPOSE

Create distinctive social-first videos combining:

* learning;
* policy ideas;
* social commentary;
* comedy;
* cultural references;
* evidence;
* animation;
* live presentation;
* media excerpts; and
* strong personal perspective.

The objective is not conventional educational content, generic influencer content, political punditry, or sanitized corporate explainer video.

The desired content should be:

sharp + intelligent + funny + provocative + evidence-aware + visually interesting + educational

Edginess is permitted intentionally.

Profanity is permitted intentionally.

Strong conclusions are permitted.

Controversial subjects are permitted.

The content should not become intellectually sloppy merely because the presentation is informal.

⸻

2. CORE EDITORIAL PRINCIPLE

Strong language. Stronger reasoning.

The content may be aggressive, irreverent, comedic, profane, skeptical, or confrontational.

The underlying reasoning should be more disciplined than the rhetoric.

Where appropriate:

CLAIM → EVIDENCE → REASONING → CONCLUSION

Do not manufacture false certainty merely to produce a stronger line.

Distinguish:

* fact;
* definition;
* evidence;
* interpretation;
* inference;
* opinion;
* value judgment;
* joke;
* speculation.

A provocative presentation should not require weak reasoning.

⸻

3. PRESENTER VOICE — INITIAL PROFILE

The presenter should sound like a knowledgeable person actually talking rather than reading professionally written educational copy.

Preserve conversational language.

Examples of natural constructions:

* “What the fuck are we even doing?”
* “Like…”
* “I mean, c’mon.”
* “Who gives two flying fucks?”
* “Are you fucking kidding me?”
* “Let’s put a pin in this giant fucking grenade.”
* “What the hell does this have to do with…?”

Do not automatically correct conversational grammar into formal written English.

Sentence fragments are acceptable.

Abrupt transitions are acceptable when they sound natural.

Rhetorical questions are encouraged.

Strategic repetition is encouraged.

Profanity should normally serve a function:

* emphasis;
* incredulity;
* rhythm;
* comedy;
* frustration;
* transition;
* punchline.

Do not insert profanity mechanically to make otherwise bland writing appear edgy.

⸻

4. INTELLECTUAL PERSONA

The presenter is NOT:

* an omniscient professor;
* a sanitized corporate instructor;
* a generic political commentator;
* a motivational speaker;
* a rage-bait character.

The emerging persona is closer to:

“A regular person looking at something ridiculous with the audience who happens to know how to break the problem apart.”

The audience should feel invited into the reasoning process rather than lectured from above.

The presenter may say:

“This is fucking stupid.”

But should ideally be able to follow it with:

“And here’s why.”

⸻

5. PRECISION OVER POLITENESS

Do not weaken accurate terminology merely because a term is controversial or offensive to someone.

Labels are analytical tools when they have meaningful definitions.

Examples include:

* fascist;
* Nazi;
* socialist;
* communist;
* authoritarian;
* racist;
* propagandistic;
* exploitative.

The standard is NOT:

“Avoid inflammatory labels.”

The standard is:

Understand the label before applying it.

Where a label materially contributes to an argument:

DEFINE → ESTABLISH CRITERIA → EXAMINE EVIDENCE → APPLY OR REJECT LABEL

A person or institution meeting the relevant criteria may be described accordingly.

A label should not substitute for analysis.

⸻

6. ATTACK THE ARGUMENT

Where practical, direct criticism toward:

* reasoning;
* evidence;
* contradictions;
* systems;
* decisions;
* policies;
* behaviours;
* arguments.

Personal criticism may still be warranted where the person or their conduct is substantively relevant.

The objective is not artificial civility.

The objective is making criticism difficult to dismiss as unsupported abuse.

⸻

7. SELF-SKEPTICISM

The content should occasionally turn its own reasoning standard back on itself.

Example pattern:

Presenter raises an attractive but unsupported explanation.

Then:

“Maybe. But unless we’ve got some fucking evidence, that’s not critical thinking either.”

This reinforces credibility and models the behaviour being taught.

The presenter is allowed to:

* acknowledge uncertainty;
* distinguish suspicion from evidence;
* correct himself;
* identify assumptions;
* change conclusions when evidence changes.

These behaviours are strengths, not weaknesses.

⸻

8. VIDEO LENGTH

Do NOT impose an arbitrary short-form duration.

The reference material ranges from approximately 36 seconds to almost eight minutes.

The operative category is:

SOCIAL-FIRST VERTICAL VIDEO

rather than:

VIDEO UNDER 60 SECONDS

Duration should follow:

* complexity;
* narrative;
* entertainment value;
* information density;
* pacing;
* platform constraints.

Remove material that does not earn its runtime.

Do not remove necessary explanation merely to hit an arbitrary duration.

⸻

9. DEFAULT FORMAT

Primary aspect ratio:

9:16 vertical

The format may combine:

* direct-to-camera presenter;
* animated learning;
* kinetic typography;
* diagrams;
* screenshots;
* headlines;
* charts;
* photographs;
* maps;
* archival material;
* television/film excerpts;
* memes;
* news footage;
* social posts;
* documents;
* visual jokes.

No single visual mode should dominate merely because it is easier to produce.

⸻

10. VISUAL GRAMMAR

A visual change should normally have a reason.

Common triggers:

NEW CLAIM

Introduce a new visual environment or evidence object.

ABSTRACT CONCEPT

Use animation or diagramming.

EVIDENCE

Show the source, headline, data, quotation, document, graph, or relevant footage.

EXAMPLE

Move from abstraction into a concrete situation.

COMEDIC RELEASE

Use cultural clip, meme, visual gag, reaction or unexpected animation.

EMPHASIS

Use zoom, crop, typography, silence, visual isolation or camera push.

TOPIC TRANSITION

Change visual language enough that the viewer perceives a new chapter.

ATTENTION RESET

Introduce meaningful visual novelty before the presentation becomes visually static.

Do not change visuals simply because a fixed number of seconds has elapsed.

⸻

11. PRESENTER AS VISUAL ANCHOR

For flagship commentary videos, the presenter can remain the narrative anchor while the visual world changes around them.

Possible treatments:

* evidence appears beside presenter;
* headlines enter the physical composition;
* diagrams build around presenter;
* background changes with topic;
* presenter becomes integrated into animation;
* screenshots expand into full-screen evidence;
* camera pushes emphasize key lines.

The presenter should not simply remain centered while unrelated B-roll plays.

⸻

12. ANIMATED LEARNING

Animation should explain.

Use animation particularly when demonstrating:

* systems;
* relationships;
* processes;
* causal chains;
* comparisons;
* definitions;
* categories;
* timelines;
* contradictions;
* conceptual distinctions.

Example:

Instead of merely saying political groups are becoming entrenched:

1. show two groups;
2. show disagreement;
3. physically move groups farther apart;
4. increase hostility;
5. show the original problem becoming visually forgotten.

The animation teaches the concept.

⸻

13. MULTIPLE ANIMATION PROFILES

Do not force every topic into one universal illustration style.

Maintain multiple compatible visual profiles.

The chosen profile may depend on:

* subject;
* seriousness;
* emotional tone;
* intended audience;
* comedic intensity;
* production budget;
* required explanatory complexity.

Reference material should inspire production quality and techniques rather than be copied directly.

⸻

14. CULTURAL MEDIA AS INSTRUCTIONAL DEVICE

Popular-media excerpts are not merely B-roll.

They may function as:

* analogy;
* punchline;
* reinforcement;
* example;
* contradiction;
* emotional reset;
* mnemonic device;
* satire;
* commentary.

Preferred pattern:

CONCEPT → PLAIN-LANGUAGE EXPLANATION → CULTURAL REINFORCEMENT

Example:

“Something can be good even if you don’t personally like it.”

Then:

Seinfeld / Newman refusing broccoli

The clip provides a second representation of the concept and makes the idea more memorable.

⸻

15. EVIDENCE AS VISUAL CONTENT

Evidence should become part of the production.

Potential evidence objects:

* headlines;
* academic research;
* official statistics;
* legislation;
* public statements;
* social posts;
* historical material;
* photographs;
* charts;
* primary documents.

Avoid merely narrating evidence where showing it would be stronger.

For accumulating evidence, consider visual structures such as:

SOURCE → SOURCE → SOURCE → PATTERN

The screen may become progressively more crowded before resolving into the conclusion.

⸻

16. COMEDIC CONTRAST

One emerging technique is deliberate movement between intellectual seriousness and absurdity.

Example:

Academic concept.

↓

Clear explanation.

↓

Ridiculous plain-English translation.

↓

Cultural clip.

↓

Return immediately to serious argument.

The contrast itself creates humour.

Do not make every moment comedic. Seriousness makes the absurd moments stronger.

⸻

17. INFORMATION RHYTHM

Useful recurring pattern:

PROVOCATION
→ EXAMPLE
→ EXPLANATION
→ EVIDENCE
→ VISUALIZATION
→ COMEDIC RELEASE
→ INSIGHT
→ MOVE

Not every section requires every stage.

Avoid prolonged stretches containing only:

presenter talks → presenter talks → presenter talks

Likewise avoid:

random B-roll → random B-roll → random B-roll

Visuals should participate in meaning.

⸻

18. EDUCATIONAL DESIGN

The audience should leave understanding something—not merely knowing the presenter’s opinion.

Potential learning mechanisms:

* definition;
* distinction;
* worked example;
* analogy;
* non-example;
* comparison;
* categorization;
* causal explanation;
* visualization;
* repetition;
* mnemonic cultural reference;
* retrieval/callback.

Whenever possible, convert abstract concepts into something viewers can recognize later.

⸻

19. CALLBACKS

Reuse earlier visual or comedic elements later in the video.

Example sequence:

* animated potato introduced during pronoun discussion;
* potato briefly returns during final montage.

Callbacks create cohesion and reward attention.

Potential callback objects should be identified during storyboarding.

⸻

20. HOOKS

Avoid generic creator hooks such as:

“Here are five things you need to know…”

Prefer hooks arising naturally from genuine frustration, curiosity, contradiction or absurdity.

Examples:

“One of the things that fuckin’ grinds me right now…”

“What in the utter fuck are we even doing?”

The hook should establish:

1. tension;
2. personality;
3. subject;
4. reason to continue.

Do not manufacture a misleading hook that the video fails to satisfy.

⸻

21. STRUCTURAL SPINE

Before writing scenes, define one controlling question or thesis.

Example:

“What the fuck happened to critical thinking?”

Every major section should ultimately support that spine.

Interesting tangents that do not support the controlling idea should be:

* removed;
* shortened;
* or retained for another video.

This permits wide-ranging examples without creating an incoherent rant.

⸻

22. SCRIPT + STORYBOARD FORMAT

Future drafts should NOT be delivered as prose treatments when a reviewable video draft is requested.

Use scene-level structure.

Each scene should support fields such as:

SCENE
Scene number/title.

PURPOSE
What this scene accomplishes.

SCRIPT
Exact or near-exact spoken language.

PRESENTER
Camera/performance direction.

VISUAL
Animation, footage, graphics, typography or composition.

MEDIA
Requested external clip/image/reference.

EVIDENCE
Claims requiring supporting material.

AUDIO
Music, silence, sound effect or clip audio where material.

TRANSITION
How the scene enters/exits where important.

CALLBACK
Any element intended for later reuse.

Not every field needs content in every scene.

⸻

23. SCRIPT DEVELOPMENT PROCESS

PASS 1 — CONVERSATIONAL DUMP

Presenter explains the idea naturally.

Do not over-structure.

Capture:

* vocabulary;
* profanity;
* sentence rhythm;
* jokes;
* frustration;
* examples;
* analogies;
* tentative ideas.

PASS 2 — FIND THE SPINE

Determine:

* central question;
* thesis;
* major supporting sections;
* learning objective.

PASS 3 — ROUGH SCRIPT/STORYBOARD

Convert the conversation into scenes while preserving voice.

PASS 4 — HUMAN VOICE REVIEW

Presenter marks:

* “doesn’t sound like me”;
* incorrect interpretation;
* missing nuance;
* excessive polish;
* stronger language desired;
* weaker language desired;
* visual ideas;
* clips;
* jokes;
* evidence requirements.

PASS 5 — REVISE

Use corrections as evidence for both:

1. the current video; and
2. future updates to this specification.

PASS 6 — EVIDENCE PASS

Identify every externally verifiable substantive claim.

Classify as appropriate:

* verified;
* needs sourcing;
* opinion;
* inference;
* joke;
* intentionally speculative.

PASS 7 — PRODUCTION STORYBOARD

Specify sufficient visual direction for production tooling.

PASS 8 — PROTOTYPE

Produce a rough version before committing to high-cost polish.

PASS 9 — REVIEW

Evaluate:

* voice fidelity;
* clarity;
* factual integrity;
* pacing;
* visual variety;
* educational effectiveness;
* humour;
* production quality.

PASS 10 — UPDATE STANDARD

Record genuinely reusable lessons.

Do not convert every one-off creative decision into a permanent rule.

⸻

24. SOURCE & MEDIA PROVENANCE

Maintain a production source log.

For external media record where practical:

* asset/clip;
* source;
* creator/rightsholder where known;
* program/publication;
* URL/source location;
* approximate excerpt;
* purpose in video;
* scene;
* credit text;
* relevant notes.

For factual evidence record:

* claim supported;
* source;
* publication date;
* access date where useful;
* source type;
* relevant excerpt/data;
* scene.

The purpose is editorial integrity, reproducibility, credit, and production management.

⸻

25. CREDITS

Provide viewer-facing credits appropriate to the medium.

Videos may contain an end-card statement explaining the use of third-party excerpts for commentary, criticism, education, parody, satire or other applicable purposes.

Detailed credits may additionally live in:

* caption;
* description;
* linked source list;
* production record.

Credit creators and sources because proper attribution is an editorial value, not merely a defensive exercise.

⸻

26. FACT CHECKING

Before production lock:

Verify consequential factual claims.

Prefer primary or authoritative sources where practical.

Do not allow:

* a screenshot without context;
* an unsourced viral claim;
* a misleading crop;
* an inaccurate statistic;
* an uncertain quotation

to become “true” merely because it improves the story.

Where evidence is contested, represent the relevant uncertainty rather than silently converting interpretation into fact.

⸻

27. EDITING GRAMMAR

Reference techniques worth testing include:

* punch-ins;
* reframing;
* rapid cuts;
* hard cuts;
* match cuts;
* visual interruptions;
* full-screen evidence;
* animated overlays;
* kinetic typography;
* compositing;
* silence before punchlines;
* deliberate holds;
* montage;
* abrupt visual resets.

Editing intensity should follow meaning.

Fast editing is not automatically good editing.

Slow moments may increase the impact of important ideas.

⸻

28. PRODUCTION TIERS

At least two production tiers appear useful.

FLAGSHIP

Higher production value.

Potentially includes:

* presenter;
* custom animation;
* sophisticated compositing;
* evidence visualization;
* external clips;
* designed transitions;
* multiple visual environments.

Best suited to major policy, learning and social-commentary pieces.

RAPID LEARNING

“Good enough” production optimized for speed.

Potentially includes:

* narration;
* simpler animation;
* stock/reference assets;
* typography;
* diagrams;
* limited custom compositing.

The intellectual standard should remain high even when production complexity is low.

Additional tiers may emerge through testing.

⸻

29. WHAT TO AVOID

Avoid drifting toward:

GENERIC CORPORATE LEARNING

Overly polished, sterile, safe and personality-free.

GENERIC INFLUENCER CONTENT

Manufactured hooks, empty gestures and algorithmic clichés.

RAGE BAIT

Provocation without substance.

RANDOM B-ROLL

Visual changes unrelated to meaning.

PROFANITY AS COSTUME

Swearing inserted merely to manufacture personality.

FALSE BALANCE

Treating every proposition as equally supported merely because competing positions exist.

TRIBAL REASONING

Starting with the desired team conclusion and reverse-engineering justification.

LABEL WITHOUT DEFINITION

Using analytical terminology without understanding what it means.

EVIDENCE DUMP

Showing ten sources without explaining what they establish.

OVER-AUTOMATION

Automating creative decisions before the underlying production grammar is understood.

⸻

30. AUTOMATION PRINCIPLE

Automation should be introduced incrementally.

Good early candidates:

* source logging;
* media inventory;
* transcript generation;
* claim extraction;
* source retrieval;
* citation tracking;
* clip metadata;
* storyboard formatting;
* asset organization;
* production checklists;
* version tracking.

Potential later candidates:

* animation generation;
* B-roll recommendations;
* clip retrieval;
* rough cuts;
* captioning;
* motion graphics;
* platform adaptation.

Do not automate a creative rule merely because one successful video used it.

First establish that the behaviour is genuinely repeatable.

⸻
